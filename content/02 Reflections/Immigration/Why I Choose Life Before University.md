in september, i will begin studying applied linguistics at universidade aberta in lisbon. at 29, i feel this is the only possible moment for this.

i attended a small private school in my final year of secondary school; there were probably around 30 students in my class. one thing from that year left a deep impression on me.

i had a geography teacher who also owned a preparatory school for university entrance exams. in every class, he would emphasize — if not outright pressure us — to fight for admission to the “best” university programs, which, in his view, were medicine, law, economics, and similar fields.

of all the dreams i had as a teenager, every one of them belonged to the arts. at one point or another, i wanted to be a fashion designer, a singer, a poet, a painter, a music producer, a screenwriter, a photographer…

by the time i was 18, though, i had already realised that, despite art always being a constant in my life, it wouldn’t be my primary profession. i had other ambitions — ones that had nothing to do with my career and depended on a certain level of financial stability. unless i stumbled upon some extraordinary stroke of luck, i knew those ambitions would probably take much longer to achieve as an artist than they would in almost any other profession.

i think my greatest dream has always been independence.

even though i didn’t need to, i started working at 17, although i’d been trying to find a job for quite some time before that. i received a small allowance, but once i started earning my own money, i wouldn’t have to answer to anyone about how i chose to spend it.

it was also a way of earning the “right” to spend most of my day away from home. i liked being away because it was through that process that i discovered who i was — even when those experiences turned out to be completely pointless.

my first job lasted a month and a half. i worked at a call centre until a creepy older man — the company shuttle driver — made it clear that, given the chance, he would sexually assault me.

my second job lasted about five months. i taught english at a daycare centre. i had school in the mornings and had to ask my teachers if i could leave class early (which was fairly common, since several of my classmates worked too).

even then, i arrived at the daycare with barely enough time to eat lunch — just ten minutes before my first class. the daycare was in another city, and my daily commute involved six different buses.

my parents never objected. if anything, i think they saw something almost heroic in it.

it didn’t take me long to realise that the maths simply didn’t add up, and things made even less sense once my paychecks started arriving late. that was where i experienced the satisfaction of quitting a job for the first time.

the dreams i had beyond my professional ambitions were, in many ways, an extension of that same desire for independence.

i wanted to live in europe again. i wanted to travel the world. i wanted to become a polyglot. looking back, i realise that, in the simplicity of my youth, i already understood that each of those dreams represented a different kind of freedom. living in europe would give me autonomy as a daughter and as a woman. travelling and learning new languages would expose me to different ways of living and, more importantly, to the realisation that those ways of life were possible.

my plan a was to leave brazil as soon as i finished high school.

my plan b became pursuing an online degree in business administration, joining an american company, and spending those four years building my career so that, afterwards, i could leave brazil with greater ease and an established career already behind me.

although i hadn’t yet decided between plans a and b, whenever i was asked about my future in geography class, i would mention plan b to better fit in with everyone else — even though it was probably still far from what people expected of me.

i vividly remember my geography teacher’s dismissive reaction to what, to me, seemed like the most sensible choice under the circumstances.

a business administration degree was already looked down upon. online education carried a stigma. i had no interest whatsoever in trying to get into a public university.

who was i to compete with the future doctors, lawyers, and economists of usp and unicamp?

that christmas, my mother was diagnosed with breast cancer at the age of forty-three. after her diagnosis, plan b became the obvious choice.

four months later, in april of the following year, i was hired by ibm as an intern.

later on, i learned, one by one, that none of my classmates had ended up achieving the futures they had imagined for themselves. most of them, in fact, also went on to study business administration at private universities with relatively easy admission requirements.

i also came to realise that my geography teacher’s disappointment in me may have had less to do with my choices than with the fact that i was never going to buy one of his prep courses.

unlike my classmates in my final year of high school, i ended up working alongside people who had actually studied at unicamp and other prestigious public universities.

it always struck me as ironic that i hadn’t put in even half the effort to end up in the same place. that isn’t to diminish their achievements in any way. i met people i came to respect immensely.

but in my case, taking the harder road simply didn’t make sense when it had never been my dream in the first place. it was merely an obligation that, eventually, would have brought me closer to the things i truly wanted.

i spent a year and a half working in a department similar to human resources at ibm.

i learned far more than excel formulas: how to sit through meetings that could have been emails; how to build social capital through appearances; and, of course, how to deal with the allergy attacks brought on by the office air conditioning.

i couldn’t see myself there.

my life wasn’t going to resemble my dream of becoming an artist, but i didn’t have to settle for the opposite extreme either: spending fifteen hours a day in front of a screen, having only three weeks of holiday each year — holidays that would probably still be interrupted by emails and unfinished work — and, ultimately, repeating the life i had watched my father live while i grew up.

by then, my mother had finished chemotherapy and recovered. i was able to be by her side throughout that painful process, and i would do it all over again.

but as that chapter came to an end, an idea i had never fully managed to let go of began to return: plan a.

ten years have passed since i left brazil.

i have never stopped working, and for most of that time i have worked in education, mainly with children. childhood — including my own — has always felt like familiar ground to me.

i never dreamed of becoming a teacher. but now i understand how everything led me here.

more importantly, i found a balance between having a sense of purpose and the financial stability i needed to pursue my other goals.

i never finished my business administration degree. and i’m genuinely glad i didn’t.

from my perspective, the hardest thing i could have done at nineteen would have been to walk away from a life that had already been carefully designed to “work” and start over from scratch in a place where absolutely nothing was guaranteed.

most people didn’t understand.

as commonplace as a university degree has become, many people still mistake higher education for intelligence.

perhaps that’s why i never felt a deep desire to go back to university. what always made me angry was having that path imposed on me as though there were no other alternative.

there was.

i found my footing gradually.

today, i find myself in a privileged position — one i probably would never have reached without some of the privileges i’ve had in life: living in europe as a child, learning english at an early age, and always being able to rely on my parents’ financial support whenever i needed it. i can’t ignore that.

at the same time, there was also something greater than myself that kept me fighting, often when i had no strength left, that kept me believing, often for no apparent reason at all.

i can also say that i have lived through inhumane situations. during my [[Immigration]] process, there were moments when i felt completely dismantled. but i found the pieces, one by one, and rebuilt myself into someone new.

i love a quote by nina simone:

> “what kept me sane was knowing that things would change, and it was just a matter of holding on until they did.”

i don’t think i’m too old to go back to university.

the truth is, i never stopped studying; i simply wasn’t enrolled in a higher education institution anymore.

i took countless french and german courses.

i also earned a cambridge teaching qualification.

i prepared so many english lessons that i probably know english grammar better than i know the grammar of portuguese, my native language.

i studied cinema.

i taught myself photography, cooking, crochet, knitting, anthotype printing, jewellery making, ceramics, collage, embroidery, and video editing.

i kept reading poetry, biographies, philosophy, and novels.

i travelled — alone and with others — to more than twenty countries, experienced different cultures, and discovered new ways of seeing the world.

i lived in five different countries.

if there is one thing i have never stopped being, it is a student. i just wasn’t a student in the way society says we’re supposed to be.

i take an almost childlike pride in knowing that i reached the life i wanted without submitting myself to a university degree that would have served only as another line on my résumé. as an immigrant, i have developed a deep resentment toward the power that a simple piece of paper can hold over people’s lives.

now i’m going to university because i want to, because i find it genuinely interesting. to me, that’s a luxury.

and if it couldn’t be that way, i wouldn’t accept it any other way.
